## 1. The real climate problem

### 完整版（约 1600 字符）
```
TeaWindow addresses compound climate risks affecting Longjing 43 tea in Meijiawu (30.21°N, 120.10°E), a core West Lake Longjing production area in Hangzhou, China. Around 70% of local tea gardens grow Longjing 43, an early-budding cultivar with relatively weak drought tolerance.

Early spring growth exposes tender shoots to late frost and sudden cold spells, while summer heat and drought can scorch leaves and affect both current growth and the following spring harvest. Prolonged rainfall and typhoons further complicate harvesting and field management.

The impacts are already documented. A major cold event in 2010 affected about 1.5 million mu (~100,000 ha) of tea gardens across Zhejiang and caused approximately RMB 1.7 billion in losses; in the West Lake area nearly 30% of tea gardens were affected and harvesting was delayed by about ten days. In 2020 and 2022, approximately 5,000 mu (~330 ha) of tea gardens in the West Lake area experienced heat damage, with some gardens reporting yield reductions of about one third. Hangzhou recorded 60 high-temperature days in 2024, a record.

The primary users are tea farmers, harvesting teams, cooperatives and tea enterprises. They need to decide when to harvest, irrigate, drain fields, provide frost protection or deploy shade. Existing weather, terrain and vegetation signals are distributed across different sources and are difficult to translate into timely, plot-level actions.
```

## 2. The specific task performed by AI

### 完整版（约 2450 字符，可能超限）
```
TeaWindow converts weather, vegetation and terrain data into daily compound-risk assessments and practical tea-garden actions.

The system calculates agricultural climate indicators including growing degree days, frost, sudden spring cold, heat stress, consecutive dry days, water deficit and prolonged rainfall. A gradient-boosted decision tree model combines temperature, humidity, soil-moisture estimates, precipitation, phenology and temporal features to identify compound risk. Ensemble forecasts provide uncertainty ranges, while terrain and NDVI data support plot-level interpretation. Transparent rules then calculate harvest suitability and recommend harvesting or maintenance actions. DeepSeek is optional and is used only to rewrite verified results into accessible Chinese; it is not the source of numerical conclusions, and a rule-based fallback is always available.

AI is necessary because a single threshold, such as minimum temperature alone, cannot capture interactions among frost, heat, drought, rainfall and crop stage. On held-out 2024–2025 data, the multivariable GBDT reproduced the expert-defined risk labels better than a single-temperature baseline (AUC 0.998 vs 0.78; F1 0.85 vs 0.50). This remains weak-label evaluation and is not presented as field accuracy.

Validation combines several layers. For spring frost during February–April 2022–2025, archived forecasts were compared with ERA5 over 357 days. Minimum-temperature MAE was 1.32°C (95% CI: 1.21–1.43), compared with 2.08°C for a persistence baseline; lead-time MAE was 0.49 / 0.67 / 0.91°C at 1 / 3 / 7 days. Frost-event POD was 0.709, FAR 0 and CSI 0.709, with 73 true positives and 30 missed events. ERA5 is reanalysis rather than tea-garden ground truth, so the next validation stage will freeze forecast versions and compare them with timestamped, plot-level sensor observations and explicit positive and negative field reports. Final recommendations remain decision support and must be checked against field conditions and official warnings.
```

Data & AI: Weather (Open-Meteo ERA5 history + MET Norway forecast, dual-source with fallback), NASA GIBS MODIS NDVI, Sentinel-2 (catalog) and plot terrain are fused into agricultural climate indicators; a numpy GBDT identifies compound risk; transparent rules give harvest suitability and windows; DeepSeek is optional rephrasing only, with a rule-based fallback. Outputs are decision support only.
